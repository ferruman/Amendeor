# English fiction editing for Amendeor

Guide: `check --guide en-fiction-editing`. Catalog version `0.3.0`.

Editorial principles for English fiction, written in our own words. Every row is a question to put to a passage; a finding is a reason for the author to reread it — never a defect by itself and never an automatic edit. None of these principles is a rule: over-applied, they strip a manuscript to an emotional minimalism that does not fit it.

This guide reads dialogue. It owns dialogue, attribution, exposition, point of view and psychic distance, the staging and logic of the scene, and the emotional honesty of the narration (explanation, sentimentality, frigidity). Line-level clarity belongs to [en-clarity](en-clarity-principles.md); diction, figures, rhythm and the reader's immersion at the level of the sentence belong to [en-prose-style](en-prose-style-principles.md).

## Boundary with Esgardeor

Esgardeor is Codicora's literary reviewer for the whole book: plot threads and their closure, setup and payoff, pacing over chapters, scene-ending monotony, character arcs and continuity of facts. This guide reads one window of a scene with its neighbouring paragraphs and judges the prose and drama of that passage only; it never asks whether the plot moves, whether a thread is paid off, whether a scene should exist, or whether a fact contradicts another chapter. A finding that needs the whole book is discarded by the prompt.

Mode: **D** — a narrow text signal exists (reported unverified as a hint); **C** — contextual judgment by the editor model, kept only if the independent verifier agrees. Severity: **medium** — a reader can lose who speaks, whose head we are in, or what happened; **low** — a noticeable weakness; **info** — advisory, close to taste.

## Principles

| ID | Name | Question | Mode | Context | Valid finding | Keep when | False-positive risk | Severity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `fiction.explained-emotion` | Explained emotion | Does the narration name or explain a feeling, or restate what a line of dialogue means, when the dialogue, a beat or the scene has already shown it? | D+C | The paragraph and the dialogue it explains | "Get out of my kitchen," she said furiously, and threw the ladle at him. — the line and the ladle already carry the fury | The line does not show the feeling (then the dialogue, not the tag, is the problem — say so); a telling sentence that adds a new note; tale-like narration that states feelings by convention; a distant narrator by design; a passage that would turn cold without it | medium | low |
| `fiction.ly-attribution` | Adverb on a speech tag | Does an *-ly* adverb on a dialogue tag carry the emotion that the spoken words should carry? | D+C | The tag and its line | "Fix the fence," he said bitterly. | The adverb describes the act of speaking itself — softly, quietly, loudly, clearly; a period pastiche or comic effect on purpose | low | low |
| `fiction.said-bookism` | Strained speech verb | Does a tag use a verb that is not a way of speaking (smiled, grinned, chuckled a sentence) or a showy substitute for *said* that pulls attention to the mechanics? | D+C | The tag and its line | "Not tonight," he grinned. | The verb names a real speech act that adds information (whispered, shouted, replied to an actual question); a period, genre or pastiche convention the book keeps consistently; the action is a separate sentence (a beat), not a tag | low | low |
| `fiction.quoted-thought` | Thought in quotation marks | Is unspoken thought punctuated as speech? | D+C | The sentence | "He's lying again," she thought. | The character actually says it aloud; a house style of the book applied consistently; a quotation the character recalls | low | low |
| `fiction.speaker-unclear` | Lost speaker | Can the reader tell who says each line? Or is attribution handled by repeated direct address used only to avoid tags? | C | The exchange, including the lines before the window | A three-way exchange where two untagged lines in a row could belong to either of two people | A deliberately anonymous voice (chorus, crowd, an unidentified caller); rapid two-person exchanges that alternate unambiguously | medium | medium |
| `fiction.name-consistency` | Shifting names | Is one character called by changing epithets within a scene, so that the reader has to stop and work out that they are one person? | C | The scene so far | Within a page the same woman is "Nora", "the widow", "Mrs. Dane" and "the tall woman" with no change in how the viewpoint character sees her | The change of name marks a change in the relationship or the viewpoint; a nickname a character uses in dialogue; two different characters | medium | low |
| `fiction.stilted-dialogue` | Stilted speech | Does the dialogue sound written rather than spoken — no contractions, fully formed answers, polysyllabic words this speaker would not use — so that every speaker sounds alike? | C | The exchange and what the scene says about the speaker | "I do not believe that would be advisable. Have you considered the ramifications?" from a teenager in a hurry | The stiffness characterizes the speaker — pompous, formal, a second-language speaker, a period setting; a formal occasion (oath, toast, testimony) | high | low |
| `fiction.expository-dialogue` | Dialogue for the reader | Do characters tell each other what both already know, or speak more fully formed information than they would, only so that the reader learns it? | C | The exchange and the scene's situation | "As you know, our uncle sold the orchard to the Pattersons in the spring of the flood." between two siblings who were both there | One character genuinely does not know it; the telling is itself the dramatic act (a confession, a briefing that matters); the speaker's habit of over-explaining is characterization | medium | low |
| `fiction.exposition-dump` | Lecture in the scene | Does a block of background, history, technical explanation or a personality sketch stop the scene for longer than the reader needs at this point? | C | The passage and the scene around it | A paragraph-long biography of a character inserted the moment she walks in | The material is in the viewpoint character's voice and concerns; the reader needs it now to follow the action; atmosphere or a digression that harmonizes with the story; a short, necessary opening in a historical or invented world | medium | low |
| `fiction.pov-slip` | Point-of-view slip | Within a scene told from one character's point of view, does the narration report another character's thoughts or feelings, or something the viewpoint character cannot know? | C | The scene from its start; who has been the viewpoint so far | After a page inside Ben's head: "Across the table, Lucy wondered why she had ever trusted him." | Omniscient narration established from the opening; deliberate head-to-head movement held at a steady distance under one mood; a scene break; inference the viewpoint character could plausibly make from what they see | high | medium |
| `fiction.viewpoint-diction` | Language outside the viewpoint | In close point of view, does the description use vocabulary, judgments or details that the viewpoint character would not have or notice? | C | The viewpoint character as the scene presents them | A nine-year-old's close-third scene describing a room as "an exercise in restrained neoclassical symmetry" | Deliberate narrative distance (a child, an uneducated character, a disturbed mind); a retrospective narrator; elevated language at a true turning point; omniscient narration | high | low |
| `fiction.thought-mechanics` | Thought mechanics against the distance | Do thinker tags, italics or first-person thought sit in a close third-person narration that would carry the thought without them? | C | The narration's distance in this scene | "Why did I come back? she thought." inside a close third-person passage | Distant narration; a short self-conscious interior exclamation; a retrospective narrator separating then from now | medium | info |
| `fiction.monologue-interruption` | Thought after every line | Does interior monologue interrupt the dialogue after nearly every line, or repeat what the dialogue already makes plain? | C | The exchange | A quarrel where each reply is followed by a sentence explaining what the listener felt about it | The character cannot say what she feels and the scene depends on it; a deliberately slowed moment | medium | low |
| `fiction.beat-overuse` | Beats that interrupt or repeat | Do physical beats break the dialogue after nearly every line, narrate every small movement, or use stock gestures (looking out the window, into each other's eyes, at one's hands, running a hand through one's hair) that tell us nothing about this character? | C | The exchange | Every line followed by a sip, a glance out the window or a hand through the hair | A beat marks a change of emotional direction; beats that set the scene's pace or give breathing space; a characterizing gesture | medium | low |
| `fiction.proportion` | Disproportionate detail | Does a minor or mechanical action get more step-by-step space than its function in the scene, so the scene slows where nothing is at stake? | C | The scene's stakes and pace | Four sentences to cross a room and pick up a ringing phone before the call that matters | The viewpoint character's attention justifies the detail (focused fight, panic); suspense built by delay; a ritual or craft the story is about | medium | info |
| `fiction.repeated-effect` | Once is usually enough | Does the passage make the same point — a feeling, a trait, a fact — two or three times in different words, so that the weaker version dilutes the stronger? | C | The paragraph and its neighbours | "She was lonely in the new house. ... The rooms felt empty without anyone. ... She had never been so alone." | Deliberate repetition for effect or a cumulative portrait; thoughts circling to show an obsessive state; refrain or rhetorical structure; a fact repeated for continuity | medium | low |
| `fiction.word-echo` | Unintended echo | Does a noticeable word recur within a few lines by accident, especially in a different sense? | C | The surrounding sentences | "...a sharp crack in the ice ... she took a crack at the lock" in consecutive sentences | Deliberate repetition, anaphora, refrain or rhythm; a plain word with no better substitute; a name or a motif | medium | info |
| `fiction.subordinated-action` | Action tucked into *as* / *-ing* | Do *as* and *-ing* openings pile up so that actions become incidental, or force two actions into a simultaneity that is impossible? | C | The paragraph | "Climbing out of the canoe, he cooked breakfast over the fire." | The actions really are simultaneous or incidental; an occasional one used to slow and weight a moment | medium | low |
| `fiction.typographic-emphasis` | Typographic insistence | Do exclamation points, italics or scare quotes in the narration try to supply an excitement or irony the sentence does not carry? | C | The sentence | "The house was *enormous*! She had *never* seen anything like it!" in third-person narration | A character actually shouting; a short italic interior exclamation; a word referred to as a word; a narrator whose voice is excitable by design | low | info |
| `fiction.figure-at-turning-point` | Figure that steals the moment | Does an elaborate figure of speech arrive at a decisive plot moment and pull attention from the event to the writing? | C | The scene's turning point | At the moment of a confession, a paragraph comparing the speaker's eyes to sapphires on a jeweller's velvet | A poetic viewpoint character; a specific, character-grounded elevation that captures the moment | high | info |
| `fiction.dialect-spelling` | Dialect by spelling | Is dialect rendered with so many trick spellings that the reader has to translate rather than hear the speaker? | C | The dialogue and its consistency | Every word of a line respelled ("Ah wuz gwine ter ...") for a minor character | An occasional dialect spelling; a voice built on word choice, cadence and grammar; an author's established convention | high | info |
| `fiction.voice-sameness` | Interchangeable voices | In this exchange, could the lines of two different speakers be swapped without loss, because they share vocabulary, rhythm and manner? | C | The exchange and what the book tells us about the speakers | A dock worker and a duchess who both say "I find that most disconcerting" | Characters of one background speaking in one idiom on purpose; a scene too short to judge | high | info |
| `fiction.unclear-staging` | Unclear staging | Can the reader tell who is present, where they are and what they are physically doing, or has the passage dropped the concrete signals the dream needs? | C | The scene from its start | Two pages of dialogue after which the reader cannot tell whether the characters are in the car or already inside the house | A deliberately disembodied voice-scene; the staging is clear from the previous window | medium | medium |
| `fiction.psychic-distance-jump` | Careless shift in psychic distance | Does the narration jump between far and close psychic distance within a few sentences without a reason the reader can feel? | C | The paragraph and the scene's prevailing distance | "In the spring of 1902 a young clerk arrived in Hull. Damn this rain, his collar was soaked through! The clerk was later recorded as a native of Leeds." | A controlled move in for intensity or out for transition; a deliberate fixed setting; a change marked by a scene break; free indirect discourse that moves smoothly between voice and narration | high | medium |
| `fiction.unmotivated-shift` | Unmotivated change | Does a character's mood, tactic or attitude change within the passage with no event, thought or gesture to account for it, so that the reader cannot believe the change? | C | The scene from its start | A shouting match in which one side suddenly speaks tenderly with no noise, memory or pause to cause it | The cause is given earlier in the scene or in the window's context; the abruptness is the point (shock, instability) and the scene treats it as strange | medium | medium |
| `fiction.narrator-explains` | Narrator explains the drama | Does the narration explain what the scene has already dramatized — the meaning of an action, a character's nature or motive, the moral of the moment — in the narrator's voice? | C | The scene so far and the explanation | After a page of a man snapping at waiters, kicking a chair and checking his watch, a sentence telling us he is impatient because he had a bad night | A philosophical or essayistic narrator whose commentary is part of the design; tale-like narration; information the drama has not given; a necessary bridge | medium | low |
| `fiction.sentimentality` | Sentimentality | Does the passage ask for an emotion it has not earned — stock response, cheap melodrama, breathless one-sentence paragraphs, rhetoric standing in for character in action? | C | The scene from its start | A child we have just met is described as all innocence and light and dies in the next paragraph, with "Then she was gone." on a line by itself | Strong rhetoric after the drama has earned it; plain statement at an earned moment; a single isolated line that lands a real event | high | low |
| `fiction.frigidity` | Frigidity | At a grave moment, does the narration reach for a trivializing, flippant or self-amused detail that shows less concern for the characters than the situation deserves? | C | The moment and its stakes | At a deathbed, the dying woman's breathing compared to a kettle that someone forgot to take off the stove | Black comedy or satire established by the book; a viewpoint character's coping wit that the scene presents as such | high | low |
| `fiction.clumsy-insertion` | Clumsy insertion | Is a detail — the protagonist's looks, a fact the plot needs — inserted by a visible device (a mirror, a convenient remark) that shows the writer's hand? | C | The passage | She caught her reflection in the shop window: long auburn hair, green eyes, a dusting of freckles. | The device is motivated by the scene and does work beyond delivery | medium | low |
| `fiction.event-order` | Events out of order | Does a sentence give events out of their order, or suspend its meaning so that the reader has to reassemble what happened first? | C | The sentence | "Reaching for the ball, she was tripped by a dog, which had slipped its leash in the excitement and run onto the field." | Deliberate disorientation as an effect; a flashback or memory clearly framed | medium | low |

## Signals

The narrow text layer marks only what a pattern can see reliably. It skips text inside quotation marks. A signal is a hint without verification; the contextual layer judges the rest.

| Signal | Principle | Matches |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `fiction.tag-emotion` | `fiction.explained-emotion` | a speech verb followed by *in* / *with* and a named emotion (*said in astonishment*) |
| `fiction.ly-tag` | `fiction.ly-attribution` | a speech verb followed by an *-ly* adverb, except adverbs of the act of speaking (*softly*, *quietly*, *loudly*, *clearly*, ...) |
| `fiction.impossible-tag` | `fiction.said-bookism` | a speech-ending comma, *?* or *!* inside a closing quote, then a subject and *smiled*, *grinned*, *chuckled*, *grimaced*, *smirked*, *shrugged*, *nodded*, *frowned*, *beamed* or *laughed* |
| `fiction.quoted-thought` | `fiction.quoted-thought` | a closing quote followed by a subject and *thought* or *wondered* |

`"Come closer." She smiled.` is a beat, not a tag, and does not match.

## Out of scope

| Matter | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| Plot, theme, endings; scene versus summary over a chapter; flashback density; scene and chapter length; duplicate characters; once-per-book effects; stereotyped villains | Structural judgments over the whole book: Esgardeor owns them (see [en-guides.md](en-guides.md#boundaries)). |
| Name-before-verb tags ("Dave said" versus "said Dave") | Inversion is common in published fiction and in period styles; a preference, not a fault. |
| Dashes for interruption, ellipses for trailing off, a new paragraph per speaker | Punctuation and layout conventions; `edit --mode proofread` and the book's house style own them. |
| Paragraph length | Layout; a long paragraph is a legitimate tool. |
| Comma splices for speech rhythm | Natural in speech; must not be flagged. |
| Sex scenes, profanity | Matters of taste and genre. |
| Metafiction and deliberately irreal styles | Deliberate departures; exceptions in the verifier rules. |
| Choice of point of view, suspense management | The author's choice and scene structure; slips within a point of view are `fiction.pov-slip` and `fiction.psychic-distance-jump`. |
| Developing a writer's voice | Not checkable in a passage beyond `fiction.voice-sameness`; protecting voice is enforced by the verifier rules. |
